The artifact uses The Big Lebowski as its clearest example: the Dude gets caught in a network of ransom, mistaken identity, missing money, and other people's interventions. In mathematical structuralism, the identity of a position can be understood through its relations to other positions rather than through some intrinsic identity of its occupant. Your cinematic extension is:

Person + Misidentification → Role → Consequences

3. Why character arcs might be the wrong default model

Consider two competing models of narrative.

Character's initial flaw
↓

↓

The plot is organized around what the protagonist becomes.

Model B · The Coen collision hypothesis
Existing roles, desires and misunderstandings
↓
Misidentification → collisions
↓
Residual consequences → altered state

The plot emerges from interactions that no single character controls.

The second model changes the explanatory question. Instead of asking whether Jerry learns his lesson, or whether the Dude develops, ask:

  • What role does each character believe they occupy?
  • What role does the surrounding network assign them?
  • Which misunderstanding causes those two positions to diverge?
  • How does one collision change the conditions of the next?
  • What remains unresolved after the apparent ending?

This also explains why mistaken identity is more than a comic device in your framework. It is an intervention that changes the system's state.

Stage III introduces the error. Stage IV amplifies it through the collision field. Stage V records what survives.

4. Joel versus Ethan: form versus collision

Your distinction between the brothers is provocative, but I'd formulate it carefully.

I would not claim that Joel has no interest in collisions or that Ethan has no visual control. Both brothers work with elaborate composition, absurdity, and unstable identities. The more defensible distinction is one of dramatic emphasis: in your reading, Joel's formal construction becomes conspicuous in The Tragedy of Macbeth, while Ethan's collision-driven absurdity becomes conspicuous in Drive-Away Dolls.

That makes the contrast testable across other films rather than a fixed psychological claim about the brothers.

5. Kaufmann, Nietzsche, and the ending that doesn't close

The second half of your artifact invokes Walter Kaufmann's engagement with Nietzsche, existentialism, and tragic optimism. It connects the absence of clean resolution to your feedback loop \(V \rightarrow I'\).

Your pentad reads that ending not as a failed character arc, but as a state transition:

I → II → III → IV → V → I′

The final state becomes the next initial state. Experience persists, but it does not necessarily produce mastery, moral clarity, or successful adaptation.

That is a more precise claim than saying the Coens reject all closure. Their films do conclude; some resolve particular plots quite decisively. The distinction is that plot closure does not guarantee existential resolution.

And this is where the Kaufmann connection works: tragic or absurd experience need not yield a tidy moral lesson to remain consequential.

6. The strongest version of your thesis

I'd put the central proposition this way:

The Coen brothers often displace character psychology as the primary engine of narrative and foreground the collision of roles, misunderstandings, institutions, chance, and material circumstances. A character becomes consequential not simply by developing an inner self, but by occupying a position in a relational structure whose consequences exceed their understanding. The ending may terminate the plot without resolving the experience.

The word often matters. This is a critical hypothesis, not a universal law of every Coen film.

And the Benacerraf connection supplies the conceptual hinge:

  • Benacerraf: identity through structural position.
  • Kaufmann's Nietzsche: the human predicament without guaranteed moral or teleological resolution.
  • The Coens: dramatic worlds in which relational structure and contingent collisions can overwhelm individual intention.
  • Ukubona: a five-stage model for tracing how an initial misidentification propagates into consequences and persists as residual state.

The synthesis is yours; the three source traditions do different work within it.

7. The falsifiable test

Here's how I'd stop this from becoming an interpretation that explains everything after the fact.

TestCharacter-arc predictionCollision-model prediction
What best explains major plot turns?Changes in motivation or self-understandingRole assignments, misunderstandings, and interactions
What happens when identity is mistaken?The error serves the protagonist's developmentThe error reorganizes relationships and propagates consequences
What explains the ending?Transformation, recognition, or failure to transformThe resulting configuration, including unresolved consequences
What would count against the model?—Films where inner psychological development consistently explains the decisive turns better than relational structure

Apply both models to a sample of Coen films. Define the coding criteria in advance, have independent viewers classify the decisive plot turns, and compare which model better explains them. Include counterexamples rather than selecting only films that fit.

One particularly useful comparison would be The Big Lebowski, Fargo, Burn After Reading, No Country for Old Men, and A Serious Man. They offer different combinations of mistaken identity, unintended consequences, chance, agency, and unresolved meaning.

My verdict: yes, the distinction you're identifying is potentially real and illuminating. But the deepest claim isn't that the Coens simply dispense with character arcs. It's that they frequently make the relational structure more causally important than the character's internal development.

In your notation, the key inequality is:

Explanatory power of role and collision > Explanatory power of character arc

That's the proposition worth testing. And if it survives the counterexamples, you've got something more substantial than a Coen Brothers aesthetic: a general model of how agents become trapped in systems whose consequences they neither designed nor fully understand.
